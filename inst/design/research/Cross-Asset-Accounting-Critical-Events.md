# Cross-Asset Accounting-Critical Economic Events

## Executive Summary

The strongest prior-art lesson is that serious systems separate at least **two different jobs** that are often conflated in backtests: producing analytically smooth return series, and reconstructing broker-like accounting state. Systems such as Zipline and LEAN mix both approaches, but they are explicit about where the mix happens: they ingest source corporate-action records, derive adjustment ratios for research/history, and also emit or apply concrete accounting effects such as cash dividends, stock dividends, splits, symbol changes, delistings, or futures cash payouts. In contrast, systems such as Backtrader, vectorbt, and many user-driven R workflows lean harder on adjusted data, generic cash hooks, or user-authored transaction records, which is flexible but easier to misuse. citeturn36view0turn7view0turn34view0turn20search0turn24view0

For ledgr, the immediate implication of LFB-001 is straightforward: **dividend-inclusive total return cannot be claimed as accounting-faithful unless dividend cashflows are represented as replayable ledger events or as faithfully derived ledger events from vendor records**. Using split-adjusted prices fixes quantity continuity but omits distributive cashflows. Using total-return-adjusted prices can fix returns analytics, but it makes fills, fees, exposure, and pathwise account balances depend on counterfactual prices rather than the prices at which the position was actually financed and traded. Zipline’s design is illustrative here: it stores dividend payout records with ex-date, record-date, declared-date, and pay-date, pays cash on pay-date, **and** separately derives dividend adjustment ratios for price history. That duality is the cleanest prior art among the surveyed frameworks. citeturn7view0turn5view2turn8search0

The second major lesson is that “event support” is not one thing. Different event families touch different parts of state. A cash dividend changes cash and realized total-return accounting but not quantity. A split changes quantity and often cost basis per unit, and may create fractional-cash residuals. A futures daily mark changes cash through variation margin without changing contract count. An option assignment mutates both the derivative position and the deliverable underlying or cash. A ticker change mutates instrument identity without necessarily changing economic ownership. A delisting may end tradability and sometimes create a terminal cash return or mandatory position close. Systems that blur these cases into generic price adjustment shortcuts tend to hide footguns rather than remove them. citeturn36view0turn32view0turn38search0turn38search3turn37search1

The third lesson is that **timestamp semantics are not optional metadata**. At minimum, ledgr will eventually need to distinguish economic effective date from strategy knowledge date. Zipline already distinguishes dividend declared, ex, record, and pay dates in its adjustment database and pays on pay-date after eligibility is earned around ex-date. LEAN distinguishes backtest delivery timing from live delivery timing for splits and factor-file readiness, and continuous futures changes are surfaced as symbol-change events when mappings roll. Freqtrade’s perpetuals documentation likewise makes funding timing operational by exposing funding timestamps and warning that absent historical funding makes backtests inaccurate. These are all examples of the same design truth: a replay engine needs more than a single “date” field. citeturn6view2turn5view2turn36view0turn37search1turn29search0turn28search6

The highest-confidence design conclusion for ledgr is therefore this: **economic events should be treated as first-class source records and interpreted into replayable ledger events, not only as data-adjustment policy**. Where the framework cannot yet model an event family, it should say so plainly and narrow its performance claims accordingly. In practice, the first tranche that most improves truthfulness is: cash dividends and fund distributions, stock splits and reverse splits, futures daily variation margin, option expiry and exercise/assignment, continuous-futures mapping/roll provenance, multi-currency cash and FX conversion provenance, and delisting/symbol-change handling. Everything else can come later, but not pretending these do not exist is itself a design requirement. citeturn36view0turn32view0turn39search0turn39search2turn37search1turn24view0

## Terminology

An **accounting-critical economic event** is any non-trade event that changes economically meaningful portfolio state in a way that cannot be reduced to an ordinary fill. The changed state may include cash balances, position quantities, unit cost basis, realized or unrealized PnL, margin/collateral, deliverable terms, or instrument identity. Examples include dividends, splits, futures variation margin, and option assignment. This definition is an inference from how LEAN, Zipline, blotter, and PMwR treat such events: either as dedicated objects/transactions or as events that require special accounting paths distinct from normal fills. citeturn36view0turn5view2turn12view3turn24view0

A **source event record** is the vendor- or venue-originated fact record before ledger interpretation. In the clearest documented examples, Zipline ingests dividends with `sid`, `ex_date`, `declared_date`, `record_date`, `pay_date`, and `amount`; stock dividends add `payment_sid` and `ratio`; splits and mergers are stored with `effective_date`, `ratio`, and `sid`. Norgate likewise exposes stable instrument identity, symbol translations, first/last quoted dates, capital-event indicators, and dividend/distribution timing conventions. These are source-side facts, not yet the full account mutation. Categories: **source/vendor event record**. citeturn7view0turn25view0turn30search0

An **interpreted ledger event** is the replayable account mutation derived from one or more source records plus current holdings. Examples include “credit \$x of dividend cash on pay-date,” “multiply held quantity by split factor and credit fractional-cash residual,” “apply daily futures cash payout from price change times multiplier,” or “close old mapped futures contract and open new mapped one under roll policy.” LEAN, Zipline, and Backtrader all contain documented machinery of this kind, though with very different degrees of explicitness. Categories: **replayable ledger event**, sometimes also **explicit cash event**, **explicit quantity event**, **explicit margin/collateral event**, or **instrument-identity mutation**. citeturn36view0turn32view0turn34view0turn37search1

A **raw price** is the observed price history before back-adjusting for dividends, splits, or continuous-futures roll seams. LEAN distinguishes raw from adjusted/total-return modes for equities and from normalized continuous prices for futures; PMwR exposes explicit helpers to back-adjust prices for dividends and splits rather than hiding that step. citeturn36view0turn37search1turn25view0

A **split-adjusted price** is a price history transformed to smooth split boundaries while preserving economic comparability across share-count changes. Zipline stores split ratios as historical adjustment factors, and Backtrader data feeds can consume adjclose-based series that incorporate split adjustments. This is a **price-adjustment shortcut** unless accompanied by explicit quantity updates for held positions. citeturn7view0turn17search0

A **dividend-adjusted** or **total-return price** is a price history altered so that cash distributions are reinvested in the series. LEAN’s `Adjusted` and `TotalReturn` modes factor dividends into price history for backtests, while PMwR’s `div_adjust` reconstructs a return-consistent series by inserting dividend cashflow into price-return arithmetic. This is analytically useful but becomes an **accounting fiction** if fills, fees, leverage, and position notional are computed on those synthetic prices. Categories: **analytics-only adjustment** or **price-adjustment shortcut**. citeturn36view0turn25view0

**Ex-date** is the date that determines entitlement for a dividend or similar distribution. Zipline documents it explicitly for dividends and stock dividends, and Norgate notes that entitlement is effective on holding the security at the close on the trading day prior to the ex-date. **Pay date** is the date cash or stock is actually distributed. **Effective date** is the date a split, merger adjustment, or similar contract/instrument change takes effect. **Known_at** or **available_at** is the time a strategy could first use the information; LEAN’s distinction between factor-file delivery timing in backtests versus live is a concrete example of why this field matters. citeturn7view0turn5view2turn30search0turn36view0

**Cost basis** is the accounting basis used to compute realized and unrealized PnL. LEAN documents weighted-average cost accounting for holdings, while ledgr currently uses FIFO lots; that difference is important because event semantics sometimes interact with retained-lot identity. **Margin/collateral event** is a non-trade mutation of usable cash or locked collateral, such as futures daily cash settlement or liquidation-driven balance changes. Backtrader and Zipline explicitly document futures cash adjustment / payout mechanics, and Freqtrade exposes liquidation-price and funding-fee behaviors for perpetuals while warning that liquidation fees are not modeled. citeturn39search9turn34view0turn32view0turn29search0

## System-by-System Notes

### QuantConnect LEAN

LEAN’s strongest prior art for ledgr is that it exposes several corporate actions as dedicated objects: `Split`, `Dividend`, `SymbolChangedEvent`, and `Delisting`. For US equities, LEAN sends split objects both in advance and when effective, automatically adjusts held positions in raw/live mode, credits fractional residual value to the cash book when needed, and adjusts unfilled orders across splits. Dividends are similarly surfaced as `Dividend` objects; in raw/live modes cash is added to the cash book, while in `Adjusted` or `TotalReturn` modes the dividend is factored into price history instead. Categories: **source/vendor event record**, **explicit cash event**, **explicit quantity event**, **price-adjustment shortcut**, **replayable ledger event**. citeturn36view0

LEAN is also unusually explicit about instrument identity. The `Symbol` abstraction survives ticker changes, and ticker changes are surfaced as `SymbolChangedEvent` rather than silently rewriting history. Delistings are surfaced as `Delisting` objects, again with advance and effective modes. That is much closer to the “source record plus interpreted event” pattern than most frameworks. Categories: **instrument-identity mutation**, **source/vendor event record**, **replayable ledger event**. citeturn36view0

For futures, LEAN distinguishes adjusted continuous-contract prices from the raw price of the currently mapped tradable contract. The continuous symbol is not itself tradable; the mapped raw contract is. As mappings roll, the engine emits `SymbolChangedEvent` objects. This is a particularly useful precedent for ledgr because it separates research continuity from tradeable identity. Categories: **price-adjustment shortcut**, **instrument-identity mutation**, **replayable ledger event**. citeturn37search1turn37search0

LEAN’s options modeling is first-class by comparison with most surveyed systems. Exercising or being assigned on an option is processed through exercise and assignment models, and assignment events have dedicated handlers. For future options, QuantConnect documentation also notes that many contracts are cash-settled and can be exercised into account cash automatically at expiry. Categories: **explicit cash event**, **explicit quantity event**, **instrument-identity mutation**, **replayable ledger event**. citeturn38search0turn38search1turn38search3turn38search2turn38search4

The main hidden assumption for ledgr is accounting basis. LEAN uses weighted-average cost accounting, not lot-based FIFO. So LEAN is excellent prior art for event taxonomy and timestamping, but weaker direct precedent for lot-faithful replay if ledgr wants retained-lot semantics. It is also easy in LEAN to accidentally use normalized prices where research convenience leaks into accounting logic. citeturn39search9turn36view0turn37search6

### Zipline and zipline-reloaded

Zipline’s corporate-action architecture is the best documented example of a dual-track design. Its `SQLiteAdjustmentWriter` stores splits, mergers, dividends, and stock dividends as source records, with dividends carrying `ex_date`, `declared_date`, `record_date`, `pay_date`, and `amount`, and stock dividends adding `payment_sid` and `ratio`. Separately, it computes dividend adjustment ratios from the close on the day prior to ex-date and stores those for historical price adjustment. Categories: **source/vendor event record**, **analytics-only adjustment**, **price-adjustment shortcut**. citeturn7view0

But Zipline does not stop at adjusted prices. In the ledger, `earn_dividends` records unpaid dividend obligations keyed by pay date, and `pay_dividends` later returns the cash payment and adds stock-dividend shares on pay date. `handle_splits` also lets the position object mutate held share count and returns leftover cash for fractional pieces. This is exactly the separation ledgr should study: one vendor/source record can generate both research adjustments and replayable accounting events. Categories: **explicit cash event**, **explicit quantity event**, **replayable ledger event**. citeturn5view2turn6view0

Zipline also documents that `current()` and `history()` return prices adjusted as of the current simulation perspective, including across splits, dividends, and mergers. That means the same framework supports both accounting and adjusted-data analytics, but the semantics differ depending on which path you inspect. This is powerful, but it is also a footgun if users do not understand when they are looking at adjusted research series rather than broker-like transaction economics. citeturn8search0turn7view0

For futures, Zipline explicitly processes cash payout for held futures positions based on price change times the contract price multiplier, and its portfolio-weight documentation states that each futures contract’s value is unit price times quantity times multiplier. Categories: **explicit margin/collateral event**, **explicit cost-basis-related valuation input**, **replayable ledger event**. citeturn32view0turn32view2turn32view3

The limitation is not conceptual but coverage. Zipline’s equities corporate actions are strong, and futures daily cash payout is explicit, but the reviewed docs are much thinner on richer non-equity event families such as funding, option deliverable adjustments, bond coupons, or crypto distributions. Delisting and asset-life behavior are more implicit through lifetimes, NaNs, and auto-close than through a richly typed delisting-return event family. citeturn8search0turn32view2

### quantstrat and blotter

blotter is important prior art because it is openly transaction-oriented. Its package docs define the scope as transactions accumulating into positions, then portfolios, then accounts, and state explicitly that transaction types include splits, dividends, expirations, assignments, and others, though some are implemented and others are not. That framing is unusually aligned with ledgr’s direction. Categories: **replayable ledger event**, **explicit cash event**, **explicit quantity event**, **implementation-specific shortcut** where coverage is incomplete. citeturn12view3

The clearest documented non-trade economic event is `addDiv`, which records a cash dividend transaction and explicitly says it does not affect position quantity the way a split would. At the same time, the man page still contains TODOs for adding transaction types and an as-of date field. That is highly relevant: the design intent is ledger-faithful, but the metadata model is visibly under-specified. Categories: **explicit cash event**, **replayable ledger event**, **implementation-specific shortcut**. citeturn11view0

`addTxn` is equally instructive. It computes value, average cost, position changes, and realized PnL and stores them in the portfolio object. Its note says the function will eventually also handle other transaction types such as corporate actions and option expire/assign, pointing again to a ledger-first architecture that never fully generalized all event families. quantstrat, as the strategy layer built atop blotter and FinancialInstrument, inherits these semantics rather than redefining them. citeturn11view1turn33search0turn16search0

The main lesson for ledgr is that a transaction-oriented architecture is not sufficient by itself; you also need a rigorous event taxonomy, timestamp model, and provenance scheme. blotter shows the direction, but the docs themselves show where ambiguity accumulates if event classes remain generic or partially implemented. citeturn11view0turn33search0

### Backtrader, vectorbt, PMwR, and the useful extra comparison

Backtrader’s documentation is strongest where exchange-style broker mechanics are directly simulated: futures cash is adjusted each iteration, commission schemes carry margin, multipliers, and credit interest, and continuous-futures research can be created by rolling data feeds together. By contrast, equity corporate-action handling in the reviewed docs is mainly data-side, through adjusted-close capable feeds and user-extensible filters, not through first-class dividend/split ledger objects. Categories: **explicit margin/collateral event**, **price-adjustment shortcut**, **implementation-specific shortcut**. citeturn34view0turn17search0turn17search1turn35search1turn35search3

vectorbt is deterministic and explicit about order records, logs, positions, asset flow, and cash flow, but the reviewed portfolio engine is order-simulation-centric rather than corporate-action-centric. Official docs show asset and cash flow series, order records with fees, and deterministic simulation loops starting from initial cash, while data adapters like Alpaca expose raw/split/dividend/all adjustment modes. I did **not** find first-class dividend, split, merger, funding, or deliverable-change objects in the reviewed vectorbt portfolio documentation. That makes vectorbt a useful example of a clean simulation core that still leaves economic-event realism largely to user policy and data preparation. Categories: **analytics-only adjustment**, **price-adjustment shortcut**, **implementation-specific shortcut**, with possible **replayable ledger event** only if users build it themselves. citeturn20search0turn20search2turn21view1turn22view0turn22view1

PMwR is especially useful because its author is unusually explicit that `btest` “does accounting”: it keeps track of transactions, positions, cash, wealth, and a trade journal, uses timestamps directly, and allows a `cashflow` function for more complex account effects. It also exposes `div_adjust` and `split_adjust` as explicit helpers rather than hiding them inside price loaders. The manual further warns about missing values from delistings and expired contracts, and even suggests working with dirty prices plus explicit accrued-interest cashflow for bond-like instruments. PMwR is therefore strong prior art for separating bookkeeping from analytics, but it remains mostly a **user-authored event-hook** system rather than a rich typed event framework. citeturn23search1turn24view0turn24view1turn25view0turn26view0turn26view2turn27view3

As one extra comparison, Freqtrade materially improves the analysis for crypto perpetuals because it documents where non-spot accounting becomes inaccurate. Its leverage docs state that funding fees are added or subtracted from trade amount, that liquidation prices can be modeled with a buffer, that liquidation fees are **not** tracked, and that missing historical funding rates make backtests inaccurate. That honesty is exactly the standard ledgr should adopt whenever an event family is unsupported or only approximated. citeturn29search0turn29search2turn29search3

## Event-Family Analysis

### Equities and funds

The minimum accounting-faithful schema for dividends and fund distributions needs more than one date and more than one amount. The surveyed prior art strongly suggests at least: instrument identity, event type, gross amount per share/unit, currency, ex-date, record-date, pay-date, declared-date if known, and source provenance. Zipline’s dividend tables already carry most of this, and LEAN distinguishes between cashbook-credit behavior in raw/live mode versus dividend-in-price behavior in adjusted modes. For funds and distributions, the same structure extends naturally to special dividends, return of capital, and capital-gains distributions by adding a **distribution subtype** and a tax/withholding component where available. Categories: **source/vendor event record**, then **explicit cash event** after interpretation. citeturn7view0turn36view0

For splits and reverse splits, the minimal schema is: instrument identity, effective date, split factor, position-eligibility rule, fractional-handling rule, and provenance. LEAN automatically adjusts held quantity by split factor and credits remaining value for invalid lot-size residuals; Zipline mutates quantity and produces leftover cash for fractions; PMwR’s `split_adjust` shows the distinct research-side transformation of a price or shares series. Categories: **explicit quantity event**, often **explicit cash event** for fractions, plus optional **explicit cost-basis event** if per-unit basis needs recomputation. citeturn36view0turn6view0turn25view0

Spin-offs, mergers, ticker changes, and delistings require a richer identity model. LEAN’s `SymbolChangedEvent` and Zipline’s merger adjustment path show why ledgr cannot treat identity mutation as a mere lookup-table issue. The minimal schema here is: parent instrument, child/new instrument if any, effective date, exchange/venue status change, ownership conversion ratio, cash-in-lieu terms, and final tradability / auto-close / terminal payout semantics. Norgate’s stable `assetid`, current-symbol translation, last quoted date, and capital-event flagging are useful vendor-side analogues. Categories: **instrument-identity mutation**, **explicit quantity event**, **explicit cash event**, and sometimes **source/vendor event record** only, depending on what the vendor actually supplies. citeturn36view0turn7view0turn30search0

The biggest equity footguns are the two you already identified. If a backtester uses total-return-adjusted prices as if they were transaction prices, then executions, fees, notional exposure, and leverage are computed on synthetic series. If it uses raw prices but ignores distributions, then total return is understated and cash balances are wrong. Split-adjusted-only analytics sit awkwardly in the middle: quantity continuity is partially addressed analytically, but distributive cashflows are still missing. ledgr’s research question should therefore not be “should we support adjusted prices?” but “for which use cases may adjusted prices be used, and which claims become invalid once they are?” citeturn36view0turn25view0turn8search0

### Crypto spot and crypto perpetuals

The surveyed systems are markedly weaker here. In the reviewed docs, I did not find mature first-class support for **airdrops, forks, staking rewards, token migrations, or burns** in the major general backtest engines. That absence is itself a useful result. The minimal accounting-faithful schema for crypto spot non-trade events is therefore mostly an inference from custody and exchange semantics: affected asset, quantity delta, cash delta if any, entitlement rule, chain/network identifier, wallet or venue scope, event effective time, and whether the event creates a new instrument identity. Exchange rebates or fees paid in asset units should be modeled as explicit asset and/or cash deltas rather than folded into trade price. Categories: **explicit quantity event**, **explicit cash event**, **instrument-identity mutation** for migrations/forks. This conclusion is high-confidence as a design direction, but lower-confidence as “prior-art support in surveyed frameworks” because documentation there is sparse. citeturn39search11turn20search2turn12view3

For perpetuals, Freqtrade is the clearest practical warning. Funding fees are part of PnL, but missing historical funding rates can make backtests inaccurate. Liquidation prices can be estimated, but liquidation fees are not tracked, so backtests under liquidation risk are not fully faithful. That implies a minimal perpetuals event model needs: instrument identity, side, notional, collateral currency, funding rate, funding interval timestamp, funding cashflow amount, margin/collateral balance effect, liquidation trigger event, liquidation fee/slippage/penalty if any, and conversion data when collateral currency differs from account currency. Categories: **explicit cash event**, **explicit margin/collateral event**, occasionally **instrument-identity mutation** for contract redefinitions. citeturn29search0turn29search2turn29search3

The core ledgr research question here is whether a general event engine should abstract these as `cash_delta`, `asset_delta`, and `collateral_delta` primitives with typed reasons, or whether perpetuals deserve a dedicated event family because liquidation, funding cadence, and cross-collateral semantics are too specific. The surveyed prior art suggests not many frameworks have solved this cleanly; that makes explicit modeling even more important if ledgr wants to make non-spot claims later. citeturn29search0turn39search11

### Futures, options, FX, and fixed income

For futures, Zipline and Backtrader show the crucial distinction: PnL realization can happen through **daily cash movement without position reduction**. Zipline applies a futures cash payout based on price change and multiplier, and Backtrader adjusts cash each iteration for futures-like instruments. LEAN adds the separate research problem of continuous-contract mapping, where adjusted continuous prices are useful for analytics but the mapped raw contract is the tradable identity. A minimal futures event model therefore needs: contract identity, root symbol, multiplier, mark/settlement price, variation margin cashflow, notice date, expiry date, auto-close date or settlement rule, roll event provenance, and delivery or cash-settlement terms. Categories: **explicit margin/collateral event**, **instrument-identity mutation**, **price-adjustment shortcut** for analytics-only continuous series. citeturn32view0turn32view3turn34view0turn37search1turn37search0

For options, LEAN’s exercise and assignment models are the strongest reviewed precedent. The minimum schema needs option identity, style, deliverable terms, expiry, exercise/assignment timestamp, exercise/assignment quantity, resulting underlying or cash movement, and post-corporate-action deliverable metadata. The specific problem ledgr should reserve for deeper RFC work is **adjusted deliverables** after corporate actions. The surveyed docs show solid prior art on exercise/assignment itself, but much thinner explicit documentation on contract deliverable rewrites after splits, spin-offs, or special distributions. Categories: **explicit quantity event**, **explicit cash event**, **instrument-identity mutation**. citeturn38search0turn38search1turn38search3turn38search4

For FX and multi-currency cash, LEAN’s `CashBook` is the cleanest prior art among the reviewed systems. It keeps per-currency balances, credits and debits them as trades occur, and surfaces missing conversion data as `ConversionRateZero` errors. That means a minimal FX event model needs: currency balance identity, conversion rate source, conversion timestamp, trade-settlement cash movement, and provenance for any synthetic or stale conversion used. Backtrader and PMwR both point to the broader truth that multi-currency portfolios and financing costs are not afterthoughts. Whether you model overnight FX rollover as a dedicated event family or as a special interest/carry cashflow, it has to mutate the cash ledger, not just analytics. Categories: **explicit cash event**, **explicit margin/collateral event** when financing is involved. citeturn39search0turn39search1turn39search2turn35search1turn27view0

For fixed income, the surveyed frameworks are the weakest as packaged solutions, but PMwR’s manual gives an important clue: work with dirty prices when appropriate, and if signals depend on clean prices, pass clean prices separately while using explicit cashflow logic to add accrued interest. That is a concise statement of the central bond-accounting split. A minimal fixed-income event model needs: coupon event with ex/record/pay if applicable, accrual schedule, amortization/principal-paydown schedule, call event, maturity repayment event, instrument identity changes if the security is called or partially redeemed, and clean/dirty valuation provenance. Categories: **explicit cash event**, **explicit cost-basis event**, sometimes **instrument-identity mutation**. This section carries more inference than some others because first-class bond-event prior art in the reviewed frameworks is limited. citeturn26view2turn24view0

Across all four families, the replay requirement is the same: ledgr needs stable event IDs, source provenance, deterministic ordering keys, and enough timestamps to distinguish when the event economically happened from when a strategy could have known it and from when the ledger should apply it. zipline’s ex/record/pay/declared dates, LEAN’s backtest-versus-live event-delivery timing, PMwR’s explicit timestamped journals, and Freqtrade’s funding timestamps all point in the same direction. citeturn7view0turn36view0turn24view3turn28search6

## Design Patterns

The **adjusted-price-only** design is the fastest path for return analytics and often the most dangerous path for accounting claims. It is efficient, easy to backtest, and often sufficient for factor research, but it collapses fills, fees, notional, margin, and corporate-action economics into a single synthetic price series. LEAN’s `Adjusted` and `TotalReturn` modes, Backtrader’s `adjclose`-based feeds, PMwR’s `div_adjust`, and Zipline’s dividend ratio calculation all show that the technique is legitimate for some questions. It is not sufficient for replayable accounting unless it is explicitly labeled an analytics-only shortcut. Categories: **price-adjustment shortcut**, **analytics-only adjustment**. citeturn36view0turn17search0turn25view0turn7view0

The **raw-prices plus explicit economic events** design is the most defensible accounting model. It keeps trade prices real, then mutates the ledger with cash, quantity, margin, and identity events as they happen. Zipline’s dividend payout tables plus pay-date cash path, LEAN’s raw-mode corporate actions and option/futures event objects, blotter’s transaction-first framing, and PMwR’s `cashflow` hook all point here. The cost is implementation complexity and stronger vendor-data requirements. Categories: **explicit cash event**, **explicit quantity event**, **explicit margin/collateral event**, **instrument-identity mutation**, **replayable ledger event**. citeturn5view2turn36view0turn12view3turn24view0

The **hybrid design with adjustment policy** is what many mature systems actually do. They keep separate views: a research view that can consume adjusted or rolled prices, and an accounting view that replays explicit events on raw trading economics. Zipline is the clearest precedent, and LEAN’s distinction between normalized continuous futures and raw mapped contract identity is another. This is the best overall design pattern for ledgr if the goal is to support both research ergonomics and accounting faithfulness without confusing the two. citeturn7view0turn37search1turn36view0

The **broker-like ledger design** centers the account rather than the bar series. blotter, PMwR, and parts of LEAN point this way: journals, cash books, transaction records, accounts, multipliers, and cash balances are first-class, while prices are one input among many. This is the design pattern that best matches deterministic reconstruction and auditable provenance, but it requires careful treatment of underlyings, instrument masters, and non-trade event semantics. citeturn12view3turn24view1turn39search0

The **analytics-only total-return design** is valid only when it is presented honestly. It can answer “what would a continuously reinvested return series have looked like?” It cannot, by itself, answer “what cash did this account have available on this date?” or “what was the actual exposure implied by the fills?” ledgr should preserve this design as an optional analytics layer, not as the default truth source for accounting-faithful claims. citeturn36view0turn25view0

## Recommended ledgr RFC Inputs and Open Questions

The first shipment priority should be the event families that most directly invalidate current performance claims: **cash dividends and fund distributions, splits and reverse splits, delistings/symbol changes, futures daily variation margin, option expiry and exercise/assignment, and multi-currency cash conversion provenance**. Those are the smallest set that materially improves truthfulness across common equities, futures, options, and FX workflows. Crypto spot distributions and perpetual funding should follow soon after if non-spot claims are in scope. Fixed income can be deferred, but only if ledgr clearly says fixed-income accounting is out of scope. citeturn36view0turn32view0turn39search0turn29search0turn24view0

The RFC should clearly separate **source records** from **interpreted ledger events**. Source records are what the vendor said happened, with dates, ratios, amounts, symbols, and provenance. Interpreted ledger events are what ledgr applied to a specific account state. This separation is necessary because the same source record may produce different ledger outcomes depending on holdings, lot state, account currency, rounding policy, and whether a position was short or long. Zipline’s dividends are the archetype here: one source payout record feeds both adjustment ratios and pay-date cash obligations. citeturn7view0turn5view2

Identity and provenance requirements should be strict. At minimum: source vendor ID, vendor event ID if present, stable instrument ID, current display symbol, event subtype, vendor revision timestamp if available, ingestion timestamp, and interpretation version. Norgate’s stable `assetid` and QuantConnect’s stable `Symbol` abstraction show why relying only on ticker text is not enough. citeturn30search0turn36view0

The RFC should also state what ledgr **must not claim** until support exists. Until dividend cashflows exist, it should not claim accounting-faithful dividend-inclusive total return. Until variation margin exists, it should not claim broker-faithful futures cash accounting. Until exercise/assignment and deliverable adjustments exist, it should not claim option-accounting completeness. Until multi-currency conversion provenance exists, it should not claim exact account-currency reconstruction for FX or cross-currency assets. Freqtrade’s explicit warnings about missing funding and liquidation-fee gaps are a good model for such honesty. citeturn29search0turn29search2

The open maintainer questions are mostly architectural. Should entitlement-style source records be interpreted eagerly into future-dated ledger events, or lazily during replay? Should ledgr treat “instrument identity mutation” as its own top-level event family? How should lot semantics interact with weighted-average-style source frameworks during import? What is the canonical time model for `known_at`, `available_at`, `effective_at`, `pay_at`, and `settle_at`? How should adjustment-policy analytics be attached so users can ask for total-return research series without contaminating the accounting ledger? And what minimum vendor schema will ledgr require before it will claim support for an event family? These are RFC questions, not implementation details. citeturn7view0turn36view0turn24view3
