# Robust Stable Parameter Region Detection in Trading Strategy Optimization

## Bottom line

Robert Pardo’s own formulation is geometric and local: do **not** trust a single sharp optimum; prefer a **broad plateau / hilltop / hypersphere** of parameter values that all deliver acceptable results. In the key passages from *The Evaluation and Optimization of Trading Strategies* he repeatedly says the shape of the optimization surface matters, that a **broad, smooth hilltop is preferable to a spiky narrow one**, and that one should search for the **broadest possible range of parameter sets** that produce acceptable objective-function values. In the examples available from Chapters 10–11, he treats this as a judgment and surface-analysis problem, not as a single canonical numeric score. I did **not** find a Pardo-authored plateau statistic or threshold in the cited passages comparable to a formal “stability index.” citeturn5view0turn6view0

Marcos López de Prado is adjacent but distinct. His work does **not** define a Pardo-style plateau detector. Instead, he and coauthors formalize the danger of selecting the single in-sample winner from many trials: backtest optimizers search for parameter combinations that maximize performance, but under weak signal this often fits noise; their Probability of Backtest Overfitting asks whether the in-sample optimum ranks below the median out of sample, and their Deflated Sharpe Ratio adjusts for multiple testing and non-normality. That body of work is best read as a warning against trusting isolated optima, not as a contiguous-region detector. citeturn10view3turn10view4turn10view5turn10view6turn11view0

For your `stable_region(min_neighbors = N)` criterion, the most faithful deterministic design is a **strict lattice-adjacency detector on a full factorial grid**. Define neighbors as the candidates one discrete step away along exactly one parameter axis; transform the metric so that “higher is better”; count how many immediate neighbors are **similar enough** to the candidate; and fail closed whenever that topology cannot be defined. The strongest influence on that recommendation is **Pardo’s own plateau / broad-hilltop formulation**, not any framework implementation. citeturn5view0turn6view0

## What Pardo and López de Prado actually say

Pardo’s text is unusually clear on the underlying idea. In Chapter 10, discussing optimization profiles, he writes that **“the shape of the optimization profile is of critical importance”** and that **“a broad, smooth hilltop would be preferable to a spiky, narrow hilltop”** because the broad hilltop is “less likely to be the result of random chance” and because performance should decline **smoothly and continuously** as parameters move away from the optimum. He then contrasts two breakout lengths and explicitly says he would **prefer the lower-performing 13-day value over the higher-performing 6-day value** because the 13-day setting had a **“much broader range of profitable values close to the optimum,”** meaning there was **more market evidence** supporting it; he closes the example with the blunt statement that **robustness is more important than peak performance**. citeturn5view0

The Chapter 11 language points the same way. Pardo says that for a one-dimensional optimization profile, the parameter should be chosen by searching for a **“broad, high plateau,”** analogous to a **broad hilltop rather than a high spire**; in two dimensions, a hilltop; and in higher dimensions, a **hypersphere**. He then states the objective as seeking the **“broadest possible range of parameter sets”** that produce **acceptable** objective-function values. That is the closest thing, in the book material I could verify, to Pardo’s own definition of a robust/stable parameter region. citeturn6view0

What Pardo does **not** appear to do in those verified passages is turn this idea into a single book-level formula such as “neighbor count ≥ k,” “local variance ≤ x,” or “surface curvature ≤ y.” His method is operationalized through **optimization profiles, sensitivity analysis, and walk-forward robustness checks**, not through one canonical plateau statistic in Chapters 10–11 as surfaced here. TradeStation’s official Walk-Forward Optimizer FAQ in fact says its WFO implements **most of the ideas** from Pardo’s 2008 book, and its tooling centers on walk-forward efficiency, cluster analysis, sensitivity analysis, and pass/fail criteria rather than a pure plateau score. citeturn28view3turn28view0turn28view1turn28view2

López de Prado’s framing is different. In *What to Look for in a Backtest*, he argues that after trying only a small number of configurations it is already plausible to find a seemingly strong backtest even when expected out-of-sample Sharpe is zero, and that with enough trials a backtest can be fit to any desired performance for a fixed sample length. In the PBO paper, he and coauthors define overfitting at the **strategy-selection-process** level: the process overfits if the strategy with optimal in-sample performance has an expected out-of-sample rank below the median; PBO is the probability of that event. In the Deflated Sharpe Ratio paper, they add that optimizers search over vast parameter combinations and can therefore inflate apparent Sharpe through selection bias. In *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*, Chapter 9 presents grid-search CV as an exhaustive search for the parameter combination that maximizes CV performance and then stresses proper financial cross-validation. The common message is that the **single best point** is intrinsically suspect after broad search; the cure is **validation against multiple testing and OOS degradation**, not a geometric plateau detector. citeturn11view0turn10view3turn10view4turn10view5turn10view6

The broader literature supports Pardo’s intuition. The **flat maximum effect** is the idea that performance often changes little near the optimum, so many nearby settings perform similarly; later commentary on the effect emphasizes that such flatness is useful because it leaves room to choose models on grounds other than headline fit. Response-surface methodology formalizes a related view by fitting local first- and second-order approximations to a response surface, studying gradients and curvature, and, in the robust-optimization extension, explicitly considering uncertainty in factors. Robust-optimization surveys generalize the same preference: truly useful optima are not isolated singular points but solutions comparatively immune to parameter drift, tolerances, or model sensitivity. citeturn38search11turn38search20turn43view1turn43view0

## Design patterns and the important trade-offs

On neighbor definition, the prior art splits into three broad families. The first is **true grid adjacency**: immediate ±1 steps along axes on a Cartesian lattice. This is the most Pardo-faithful interpretation, because his prose and examples are surface-based and local: broad hilltops, nearby profitable values, smooth degradation away from the optimum. The second is a **radius ball** in normalized parameter space, which is natural in response-surface and sensitivity work but requires scale choices. The third is **k-nearest neighbors**, which is convenient on sparse numeric clouds but changes the semantics from “nearby on the designed grid” to “nearest among whatever happened to be sampled.” quantstrat’s warning that random subsampling introduces voids and makes surface analysis difficult is especially relevant here: once the grid is sparse, a topological neighborhood is no longer obvious. citeturn5view0turn6view0turn15view0turn43view1

On distance and normalization, mixed-type distances such as **Gower** are the standard generic answer. CRAN’s `gower` package and `cluster::daisy(metric="gower")` both exist precisely because the original variables may be of mixed types, collapsing numeric, binary, nominal, and ordered information into one dissimilarity. That is powerful, but it is not automatically Pardo-faithful: once you move to a generic mixed-type metric, two points can be “close” without being adjacent in the scan topology, and the meaning of “one step away” is lost. For a detector whose semantics are explicitly attributed to Pardo’s plateau idea, that is a significant conceptual change. citeturn44search3turn33search1turn33search0turn44search0

On irregular and sparse grids, the literature is thin and contested. Response-surface and robust-optimization methods are comfortable with irregular experimental designs because they fit surfaces or metamodels over continuous domains. But that move requires modeling assumptions, smoothing choices, or search heuristics, which your brief expressly rejects by requiring a deterministic, evidence-only detector that **never recomputes from trades** and **fails closed** when the neighborhood structure cannot be defined. For your use case, the right dividing line is therefore not “can I invent a neighborhood somehow?” but “is there a unique, auditably defensible neighborhood implied by the already-computed candidate set?” If not, error out. That is stricter than much of the optimization literature, but it is aligned with your reproducibility requirement and with quantstrat’s own caution about voids. citeturn43view1turn43view0turn15view0

On what counts as a “good neighbor,” there are several legitimate choices. Pardo’s own language supports two of them. One is a **global acceptability threshold** on the metric, because he speaks about the broadest range of parameter sets producing **acceptable** objective values. The other is a **candidate-relative similarity threshold**, because he also emphasizes that nearby values should be **similarly good** and that performance should decline smoothly away from the optimum. In commercial tools, the analogous choice is often externalized into the chosen optimization target plus walk-forward pass/fail criteria rather than embedded into a local neighborhood score. AmiBroker’s “optimization target” field and TradeStation’s custom test settings exemplify that pattern. citeturn6view0turn5view0turn26view0turn28view2

On the stability statistic itself, a simple **neighbor count** is the cleanest translation of “surrounded by nearby acceptable alternatives.” That said, broader fields also use local variance, gradient, curvature, smoothed neighborhood mean, density-like counts, and response-surface flatness. The best compromise for your API is to make **neighbor count the public decision criterion** and compute a **continuous audit score** as a companion diagnostic; this keeps the semantics Pardo-like while still preserving richer evidence for later inspection. citeturn5view0turn6view0turn43view1turn43view0

## Recommended detector design

The recommendation is a **strict, lattice-based, deterministic stable-region detector** with a count threshold and a fail-closed topology check.

Assume the input is a set of parameter tuples and one scalar metric per tuple, plus one piece of metric metadata: whether **higher is better** or **lower is better**. Transform the metric into a score \(s\) so that larger is always better. Then require the candidate set to satisfy all of the following before scoring begins: each parameter tuple is unique; every tuple has exactly one metric value; every parameter axis is ordered; and the total candidate set is the **full Cartesian product** of the sorted level sets on each axis. Numeric, integer, date-like, logical, and explicitly ordered-factor axes are admissible. Unordered categorical axes are not, unless the caller supplies an order. If any Cartesian combination is missing, if duplicate tuples exist, if all axes collapse to a single point, or if any axis lacks an order relation, the detector should return an **error**, not a guess. This is the principled fail-closed rule. It is directly motivated by your requirement set and by the fact that once the lattice is incomplete, the “plateau neighborhood” is no longer uniquely defined. citeturn15view0turn5view0turn6view0

With that topology established, define the neighborhood of a candidate \(i\) as the set of tuples that differ from \(i\) by **exactly one level index on exactly one axis** and are identical on all others. In other words, use **Manhattan distance 1 in level-index space**, not Euclidean distance in value space. This makes the detector insensitive to heterogeneous units and eliminates all normalization arguments. Unequal spacing on a numeric axis is fine, because Pardo’s “one step away” idea is ordinal on the scan, not metric in raw parameter units. This is the single biggest design choice, and it is the place where the recommendation is most clearly driven by Pardo rather than by general nearest-neighbor literature. citeturn5view0turn6view0

To decide whether a neighbor is “good,” use a **data-derived similarity band** based only on already-computed evidence. Compute

\[
\tau = \operatorname{median}\{|s(u)-s(v)| : v \in N(u),\ u<v\},
\]

the median absolute score difference across all adjacent pairs on the lattice. A neighbor \(v\) counts as supportive for \(i\) if

\[
s(v) \ge s(i) - \tau.
\]

This is deterministic, scale-free in the sense that it is expressed in the metric’s own observed adjacent-step units, and generic across Sharpe-like and drawdown-like objectives once the direction is normalized. It is not found verbatim in Pardo, but it is the cleanest way to operationalize his “similarly good” / “smooth degradation” language **without introducing a second user-facing knob** or requiring domain-specific profitability thresholds that many metrics do not possess. citeturn5view0turn6view0

The decision rule should then be:

\[
\text{eligible}(i) \iff \#\{v \in N(i) : s(v) \ge s(i)-\tau\} \ge \texttt{min\_neighbors}.
\]

This makes `min_neighbors` the public control, which matches the semantics of “surrounded by enough nearby support.” At the same time, the detector should always record two continuous diagnostics for auditability: the **support ratio** \( \#\text{good neighbors} / \#\text{available neighbors} \) and the **local smoothness range** \( \max_{v \in N(i)} s(v) - \min_{v \in N(i)} s(v) \). Those diagnostics should not change pass/fail by default, but they are extremely useful in post-hoc review. citeturn5view0turn6view0

This design is intentionally conservative. It does **not** try to rescue sparse grids with kNN, does **not** use Gower on unordered mixed types, does **not** smooth the surface, and does **not** infer missing candidates. That conservatism is a feature, not a bug, for a detector whose remit is deterministic reproducibility and failure-closed behavior. If later you want a broader “experimental robustness score” for sparse or mixed-type spaces, that should be a **different criterion**, not a silent extension of `stable_region()`. citeturn15view0turn33search1turn44search3

Two implementation notes matter in practice. First, edge and corner candidates have fewer available neighbors. That should **not** cause an error; it should only mean that large `min_neighbors` values are harder for boundary candidates to satisfy. Second, because `min_neighbors` is an absolute count, it is most interpretable when chosen with dimensionality in mind. As a practical default, values around the number of free axes are easier to justify than very large counts, but the rule itself should remain literal and deterministic. citeturn5view0turn6view0

The **single strongest influence** on this recommendation is **Pardo’s own “broad plateau / broad hilltop / broadest possible range of acceptable parameter sets” formulation**. TradeStation’s WFO is the strongest software influence, but it shapes the recommendation only secondarily by showing that commercial practice often validates robustness through repeated support, sensitivity, and consistency checks rather than through the single best point. citeturn6view0turn28view0turn28view2

## Audit trail and API semantics

For auditability, the detector should persist enough metadata that an independent reviewer can reconstruct every decision without rerunning the backtest. At minimum that means: metric name; optimization direction; parameter names; sorted level sets per parameter; verified grid shape; candidate count; adjacency rule; the derived global tolerance \( \tau \); the requested `min_neighbors`; and, per candidate, the available-neighbor count, good-neighbor count, support ratio, local smoothness range, and eligibility bit. quantstrat’s emphasis on auditable parameter runs and audit environments is a helpful precedent here. citeturn15view0turn15view1

On the API question of **count threshold versus continuous score**, the best answer is “both, but in different roles.” The public pass/fail criterion should remain `min_neighbors`, because that matches the Pardo semantics your criterion is supposed to embody and keeps the behavior easily explainable. The continuous score should be logged and surfaced, but not made the primary contract unless you are prepared to support bandwidth-like choices, calibration questions, and more ambiguity about what “stable” means. In other words: make the detector **discrete in decision, continuous in audit**. citeturn5view0turn6view0turn28view3

## R ecosystem choices for an implementation

If you adopt the recommended strict lattice detector, the core implementation does **not** need an external dependency. The main operations are Cartesian-grid validation, index construction, adjacency lookup, and counting. Those are simpler, more transparent, and more reproducible in a small native implementation than through a nearest-neighbor or mixed-distance package. The reason is not that those packages are poor; it is that they solve a **different problem**. FNN and RANN solve fast nearest-neighbor search in numeric spaces, while `gower` and `cluster::daisy` solve general mixed-type dissimilarity. Your recommended detector does neither. citeturn36view0turn35view0turn32search5turn33search1

## Open questions and limitations

The most important limitation is that Pardo’s own verified discussion is **conceptually precise but numerically underdetermined**. I found strong, primary-source support for the plateau idea, the preference for broad neighborhoods over isolated spikes, and the broadest-range-of-acceptable-values criterion, but I did **not** find a Pardo-authored formula that uniquely dictates a neighbor-count threshold, a tolerance band, or a specific local statistic in Chapters 10–11. Any such detector is therefore an **interpretive implementation**, not a direct transcription of an explicit Pardo equation. citeturn5view0turn6view0

A second limitation is documentation accessibility. The MultiCharts help pages that appear highly relevant in search results returned **403** when fetched directly, so I was able to verify only the search-engine snippets, not the full official text. I therefore treated MultiCharts as suggestive evidence, not as a decisive source for detector semantics. citeturn25search0turn25search2turn24view0turn24view1

A final boundary to note is that the recommended detector is intentionally **narrower than the full field**. It refuses unordered categorical axes and sparse non-factorial designs because the neighborhood topology is ambiguous there. That is a deliberate design choice driven by your determinism and fail-closed requirements. If you later need support for those cases, the safest path is to add a **separate** criterion with explicitly different semantics rather than silently broadening `stable_region()`. citeturn15view0turn33search1turn44search3
